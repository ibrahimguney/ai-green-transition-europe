# Conclusion

This study examined whether the rapid diffusion of enterprise AI is already associated with measurable changes in the GHG intensity of European economic activity. By linking Eurostat enterprise ICT data to official air-emission intensity accounts at the country × NACE sector × year level, the analysis provides a direct adoption-based complement to a literature that often measures AI through patents, technological-development indices, or other long-run proxies.

Two pre-specified propositions were evaluated. The first proposed that higher enterprise AI adoption would be associated with lower GHG intensity. The second proposed a U-shaped digital rebound relationship in which environmental gains at lower adoption levels would weaken or reverse as AI use expanded. Neither proposition was supported by the current evidence. Across baseline and controlled fixed-effects specifications, the AI coefficient remained statistically imprecise. The quadratic specification did not produce a robust U-shaped pattern, and excluding France and Sweden did not materially alter the conclusion.

The machine-learning results strengthen rather than contradict this interpretation. XGBoost achieved strong out-of-country predictive performance for log GHG intensity, indicating that the dataset contains substantial systematic structure. Yet grouped SHAP analysis ranked AI adoption below NACE sector, real GDP per capita, renewable-energy share, and R&D intensity. Thus, the weak econometric AI signal cannot be attributed simply to an overly restrictive linear model. Over the short adoption period currently observable, sectoral and energy-economic conditions appear to be more important for explaining variation in carbon intensity.

The main policy implication is that AI diffusion should not be treated as an automatic decarbonisation strategy. Digitalisation and climate policy are complementary only under appropriate production and energy conditions. Policies that encourage enterprise AI adoption may generate productivity and operational benefits, but environmental gains are more likely when AI deployment is accompanied by clean electricity, energy-efficiency standards, sector-specific implementation strategies, and improved reporting of compute-related energy use.

The study also highlights a measurement agenda for future research. Enterprise AI adoption, AI operational intensity, and AI infrastructure intensity should be distinguished explicitly. Future Eurostat waves may allow the panel to be extended sufficiently to examine lags, dynamic adjustment, threshold behaviour, and heterogeneous effects across sectors. Linking adoption data to measures of cloud dependence, data-centre electricity demand, firm size, digital intensity, and sector-specific energy use would further clarify the mechanisms through which AI affects environmental performance.

The findings are therefore best interpreted as an early-diffusion result. In the currently observable European panel, there is no robust evidence that enterprise AI adoption independently lowers GHG intensity and no robust evidence of the pre-specified U-shaped digital rebound effect. This absence of a strong short-run signal is itself informative: the environmental consequences of AI appear to depend less on adoption alone than on the structural, technological, and energy systems in which AI is embedded.
