# Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) is diffusing rapidly through European business while the decarbonisation of production remains a central economic and policy objective. Eurostat reports that 20% of EU enterprises with at least 10 persons employed used AI technologies in 2025, compared with 13.5% in 2024, 8.1% in 2023, and 7.7% in 2021 [@Eurostat2025AI20]. This acceleration has intensified a fundamental sustainability question: does enterprise AI adoption translate into measurable reductions in the greenhouse-gas (GHG) intensity of economic activity, or can the additional energy and infrastructure demands of digitalisation offset potential efficiency gains?

The answer is theoretically ambiguous. AI can reduce environmental pressure by improving forecasting, process control, predictive maintenance, logistics, energy management, and resource allocation. These mechanisms can lower energy and material requirements per unit of output. At the same time, AI is supported by energy-intensive computing infrastructure. The International Energy Agency (IEA) estimates that data centres accounted for about 1.5% of global electricity consumption in 2024 and documents rapidly increasing power demand associated with AI-related workloads [@IEA2025EnergyAI]. Its 2026 update further emphasises that efficiency per AI task is improving quickly while overall use, model capabilities, and energy-intensive applications are expanding at the same time [@IEA2026KeyQuestions]. The net environmental effect of AI is therefore an empirical question rather than a technological certainty.

Recent empirical research has produced mixed and often nonlinear findings. Wang, Li and Li [@WangLiLi2024], using a long international panel and dynamic-panel methods, report that AI development is associated with lower carbon emissions and ecological footprints and with stronger energy transition. Zhang, Wang and Li [@ZhangWangLi2025] identify an inverted-U relationship in a 62-country panel, with AI initially increasing emissions but eventually contributing to reductions as technological development advances; renewable energy shifts the turning point. In contrast, Alnafrah [@Alnafrah2025] reports a digital rebound effect in which high AI intensity is associated with worse environmental performance, particularly under weaker environmental-policy conditions. These apparently conflicting results indicate that AI's environmental consequences may depend on how AI is measured, the maturity of adoption, the energy system, industrial structure, and the time horizon of analysis.

This study contributes a different empirical perspective. Rather than proxying AI through patents, investment, scientific output, or composite technological-intensity indices, it uses **directly observed enterprise AI adoption** from Eurostat's ICT survey. These data are linked to official Eurostat GHG-intensity accounts at the **country × NACE Rev. 2 sector × year** level. The resulting design asks a deliberately narrow but policy-relevant question: is the rapid diffusion of AI already associated with measurable changes in the GHG intensity of European economic activity?

The study makes four contributions. First, it constructs an exact-NACE panel that avoids mechanically averaging incompatible emission-intensity ratios across overlapping sector aggregates. Second, it combines country-sector and year fixed effects with renewable-energy share, real GDP per capita, and R&D intensity, while treating the short three-wave AI panel as associational rather than causal. Third, it pre-specifies and tests a nonlinear digital-rebound hypothesis through an AI-squared specification and robustness checks excluding France and Sweden, for which Eurostat documents a 2023 break in the enterprise ICT series [@Eurostat2025AI]. Fourth, it complements econometric estimation with XGBoost, country-grouped cross-validation, and grouped SHAP analysis to examine whether nonlinear predictive evidence reveals a stronger role for AI than the fixed-effects estimates suggest.

The empirical findings are deliberately cautious. Across baseline and controlled fixed-effects models, enterprise AI adoption is not significantly associated with lower GHG intensity. The hypothesised U-shaped digital rebound effect is also unsupported. In contrast, the machine-learning model predicts out-of-country variation in GHG intensity strongly, but grouped SHAP importance assigns substantially greater predictive weight to NACE sector, real GDP per capita, renewable-energy share, and R&D intensity than to AI adoption. Together, these results suggest that, over the short period currently observable, structural sectoral and energy-economic conditions dominate the measurable relationship between AI diffusion and carbon intensity.

This conclusion should not be read as evidence that AI has no environmental consequences. Instead, it indicates that direct enterprise adoption, as currently measured, does not yet display a robust independent association with sector-level GHG intensity in the available European panel. The distinction between AI **adoption**, AI **operational intensity**, and AI **infrastructure intensity** is therefore central to future research and policy evaluation.
